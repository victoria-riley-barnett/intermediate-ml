Machine learning architectures

Eleven plates: where the mechanisms came from, what each one computes, and what it costs.

The transformer built in weeks 10 to 12 is one member of a family. Every member answers the same question — how does a fixed number of weights meet data whose shape keeps changing? — with a different arrangement of the same few parts, and every arrangement charges something for what it buys.

Nothing on these plates is new today. They are drawn to be checked against code rather than admired: each one carries its shapes, its operations as equations, the PyTorch lines that implement them, and a table that spells out every symbol. The numbers in a drawing come from the generator that sits beside it.

The last two pages announce the final project and assign its first part, the dataset choice. Everything before them is background for the decision that assignment asks for.

Where the idea came from

Seven decades, one question

The rows in these three plates name mechanisms, not papers: count the inputs and fire; move a weight by the error; reuse one filter at every position; push the error backwards; add to a state instead of overwriting it.

Two things are worth reading off the plates before the family arrives. The first is the key at the top of each one. A white box owns weights that were fitted. A grey box owns numbers that never are — a fixed window, a threshold, a pool. The same division runs through the work in this course. A white box is something training can revisit. A grey box is a decision the modeller made and froze, and no amount of training will reconsider it. Most failures in this course were a grey box nobody had named.

The second is what 1969 settled, because it is usually misread. One flat cut cannot separate the four cases of XOR and two cuts can; that part is arithmetic. What 1969 could not do was set the weights of the second cut. The plate's own words are nothing in 1969 could set that second layer's weights, and the fix is not a new idea about networks — it is a procedure, which arrives in 1986 and is still the procedure in use.

Where the idea came from

I

Architectures, 1943–1997

Read the rows in order. The first two fit a handful of numbers with procedures set by hand. The third and fourth move a weight by the error — each weight moves by the error times its own input — and that is the whole of learning in this plate.

1969 draws a limit and states it precisely. Parity of n bits needs one unit wired to all n inputs; no flat cut of the input space does the job with fewer. The row does not say a second layer would not help. It says nothing in 1969 could set that layer's weights.

The last three rows are the ones this course builds with. Backpropagation computes the error's derivative for every weight in one backward pass, at roughly the cost of the forward pass. A convolution reuses one filter at every position, so one filter detects one pattern and a layer needs many. An LSTM adds to a cell state instead of multiplying it, because a gradient flows through sums and dies in products.

Nothing in this plate explains why the fifteen years after 1997 add no architecture at all. The next plate is about what filled them.

Where the idea came from

II

The quiet years

The gap is not empty. It is a decade and a half in which the field got better at the middle column — hand-built features, then a simple model. Support vector machines, boosting and forests all arrived inside it, and on the data then available all three beat a small network.

The bottom row is the argument, and it is worth taking slowly. Three things were needed: a training set large enough that memorising it paid, the arithmetic to fit many layers, and a task nobody could hand-engineer. None of the three was new in 2012. The network and its training rule were in place by 1986; CUDA arrived in 2007 for other reasons; a crowdsourced labelled dataset existed by 2009.

So the row that ends the plate is not a leap. 2012 put the three pieces on one problem, and the ideas that had been sitting there since 1986 finally paid. That is what to carry into a project of your own: an architecture is a claim that a mechanism earns its cost, and the cost falls when data and compute fall with it.

2012 added no idea; it put all three on one problem.

The family

Eight members, one grid

Every plate in this section has the same anatomy, and the anatomy is the point. A header states the model's shapes and constants. A term table gives each operation as an equation. A PyTorch listing gives the same operations as code, with a comment naming what each line is for. A symbol table says what every letter is and what shape it has.

That repetition is itself a claim: an architecture is small enough to hold on one page, and it is checkable. A shape in the drawing has to match a shape in the code, or one of them is wrong.

The eight are ordered by what they do with a fixed number of weights facing input whose length is not fixed: multiply it, slide a window over it, carry a state across it, compress it, look it up, or let positions compare against each other. The last plate stacks the one before it.

IV

A feed-forward network

A feed-forward network is the plate the rest of the course stands on: three Linear layers, two ReLUs, and a softmax that is written outside the nn.Sequential because it is an operation and not a layer.

The parameters are worth counting once, by hand. 512x256 plus 256; 256x256 plus 256; 256x10 plus 10 — 199,690 numbers, all of them fitted, none of them chosen. That is what "white owns weights" costs at this size.

The plate also carries the limit the other seven exist to answer. x is B x 512: a row of five hundred and twelve numbers is the whole of what this network can see. No order, no length, no repetition, no notion that two rows arrived from the same sequence. Every other member of the family is a decision about data that is not shaped that way, and each one pays for the decision in parameters, in work, or in information it throws away.

d = 512 · h = 256 · K = 10

three Linear · two ReLU

softmax is output, not a layer

VI

One convolution position

A convolution is the same nine weights at every position. That sentence is the plate: one filter slid across the image, nine products and a sum at each stop, then ReLU, then a 2x2 pool that halves both dimensions.

Two costs are drawn. The window is fixed, so a 3x3 filter sees 3x3 and a pattern twice that wide needs a second layer or a larger kernel. And at the border the window runs off the image: with no padding those inputs are missing and the output there is undefined, which is why the listing passes padding=1 rather than letting the edge decide.

The flatten is where the drawing stops being an image. 64x2x2 becomes 256 numbers in an order the next Linear layer will treat as arbitrary, so anything positional has to have been captured before that line and not after it.

Plate VI is the arithmetic to check plate V's code against: one window, nine products, one number. The filter is a vertical edge detector, and the sum comes out negative three times running because the same three columns repeat.

The last line is the honest one. Work the count for one output and the window either fits inside the image or it does not; where it does not, those inputs are missing and the value is undefined rather than zero. Padding is the choice that fills them, and a choice made by the modeller is what every plate in this set draws in grey.

one filter reused at every position

32 filters, then 64

8x8 → 4x4 → 2x2 → 256

VII

A recurrent network

A recurrent cell is a small network applied T times with one extra input: the state from the step before. The same Wx, Wh and Wy are used at every step, so a sequence of any length costs the same parameters, and the plate's T = 4 is a choice about how much of the sequence one call sees.

The recurrence buys what the plate before it could not: positions reach each other through the state, so a pattern wider than one window is available without deepening a stack. It charges twice. The work is sequential — step t needs step t−1 — so the T steps cannot be run in parallel. And the gradient arriving at step 0 has passed through T products of Wh, which is exactly the multiplication that the LSTM row in plate I replaces with an addition.

h−1 = 0 is not a fact about sequences. It says the model begins knowing nothing, so the first step's output is the least informed one it will produce.

three weight matrices, used at every step

h: 256 numbers · d: 512

T sequential steps cannot be parallel

VIII

An autoencoder

The target is the input. That one line is the whole architecture: x goes in at 512 numbers, comes back at 512 numbers, and the loss is the mean squared difference between them. No label enters the loss, which makes this the only plate in the set that trains on data nobody has annotated.

The middle is the interesting part. The code z is 64 numbers, so an encoder that redraws 512 through 64 has had to decide what to keep — and the decoder trains at the same time, so the pair can settle on a code that is useful to itself rather than to a question asked later.

That is the limit to state out loud. A low reconstruction error says the 64 numbers carry enough to redraw the input roughly. It says nothing about whether they carry the thing a label is about, and a code that is excellent at redrawing a curve can be worthless at telling two kinds of curve apart.

target t = x, not a label

d 512 → h 256 → z 64

loss = mean squared residual

IX

An embedding lookup

An embedding is a table with one row per distinct token, and the lookup is by index rather than by multiplication: row 4 is row 4. The six-row, eight-column table in the plate is the entire module. The tensor that comes out is B x T x d, because a lookup keeps the shape of the indices and adds the width of a row.

This is the plate that closes the loop on week 9. There, a vector per symbol went through a separate weight for each of twelve slots, so a shift in padding changed which weights read which symbols — a dependence the training loss never reported. Here the same row is fetched at every position. Position is not in the table at all, which is the fix and also the new problem.

Two costs. The table holds vocab x d parameters, so vocabulary becomes a memory decision: a rare token costs a full row whether it appears once or ten thousand times. And a lookup has no notion of where it happened, so anything positional has to arrive from somewhere else; nothing in this plate supplies it.

vocab 6 · d 8 in the drawing

x = E[ids] · BxT → BxTxd

no position in the table

X

One attention head, by hand

Four positions, four numbers per head, one head, one batch. The plate works the arithmetic at a size where every number can be checked by hand: sixteen scores, each divided by the square root of the head width, each row softmaxed until it sums to 1.00, then each output row is those weights applied to V.

What the operation does is make each position's output a mixture of every position's value, with the weights decided by that position's query against every key. Nothing is sequential: all four positions are computed at once, which is the cost the recurrent plate paid and this one does not.

Three properties are worth naming before touching the code. The row sums are 1.00 by construction, so the output is a weighted average and cannot exceed the largest value it mixes. The scale by the square root of the head width is what keeps the scores small enough for a softmax to stay soft. And the map is dense: T positions give T x T scores, so doubling the length of the input quadruples the work at this step — the sentence the second timeline plate already carries about the transformer.

T = 4 · head width 4 · one head

S = QKᵀ · A = softmax(S/√d) · Y = AV

every row of A sums to 1.00

XI

One encoder block

The block is the two sublayers from the plates before it plus two things those plates did not have. Each sublayer sits inside a residual branch, so the stream carries x + F(LN(x)) rather than F(x): the block learns a correction to what arrives, and the untouched input travels alongside it. And the layer norm is inside the branch, before the sublayer, which is the arrangement that stacks.

The shapes are the reason this repeats. B x T x 512 enters and B x T x 512 leaves; attention mixes across positions without changing width, and the feed-forward layer widens to four times d and returns. Six copies of a block whose input and output shapes agree is a stack, and L = 6 counts those copies rather than saying anything about the mechanism.

What the block cannot do alone is in what it does not change. On its own the attention row is permutation-blind: reorder the positions and the mixture reorders with them, so order has to arrive from outside. And nothing in the block looks past the sequence it is handed.

d 512 · 8 heads · d/h 64 · L 6

x + F(LN(x)), norm first

shapes in = shapes out
